Project 4 · CS180 · Fall 2026

Image Warping
and Mosaicing

In Part A I stitched photos into panoramas. I matched points between photos, solved for the homography that maps one onto the other, warped the images with my own inverse warping code, and blended them into one mosaic. I also used the same warp to rectify a chessboard and a building so they look like they're seen head on.

  • homographies by least squares
  • nearest neighbor and bilinear warping from scratch
  • 3 mosaics plus a cylindrical one

Part A · A.1

Shooting the pictures

A homography only lines two photos up perfectly if the camera turns without moving, or if the scene is flat. So for each set the camera stays in one spot and just rotates, with a lot of overlap between shots.

Gateway is mine. I took it inside Gateway on my iPhone 16 Pro at 1×, turning the phone between shots. I took four, but the last one was almost the same view as the third, so I used three. The middle shot is landscape and the other two are portrait. The other two sets came from the internet, so I'd have three mosaics with different amounts of overlap.

Part A · A.2

Recovering homographies

A homography H is a 3×3 matrix that maps a point (x, y) in one image to (x′, y′) in another: [wx′, wy′, w] = H·[x, y, 1]. It has 9 numbers, but scaling all of them by the same amount gives the same mapping, so I fix the bottom right one to 1 and solve for the other 8, called h1 to h8.

Dividing out w and multiplying through gives two equations per point that are linear in h1 to h8:

[ x  y  1  0  0  0  -x·x'  -y·x' ]   [h1]     [ x' ]
[ 0  0  0  x  y  1  -x·y'  -y·y' ] · [h2]  =  [ y' ]
                                     [..]
                                     [h8]

Four points give exactly 8 equations, which is just enough, but my clicks are never perfect. So I used 7 to 13 points per pair, stacked them into a 2n×8 system Ah = b, and solved it with least squares (np.linalg.lstsq).

For picking points, I clicked rough matches by hand and then refined each one automatically. I take a 31×31 patch around the point in the first image, slide it around nearby in the second image, and keep the spot with the highest normalized cross-correlation, with a parabola fit through the peak to get sub-pixel accuracy. If even the best match was weak, I threw the point out. Those were usually on blank walls or repeating patterns like rows of windows, where lots of spots look the same.

Here's what the system looks like for Gateway 1610 → 1609. That pair has 10 points, so A is 20×8. The first point is (92, 542) in 1610, which matches (420.61, 390.93) in 1609, and it gives these two rows:

[  0    0  0  92  542  1  -35965.56  -211884.06 ] · h  =  [ 390.93 ]

Those bottom numbers look tiny, but they multiply pixel coordinates in the hundreds or thousands, so they still bend the image a lot.

I also tried normalizing the points before solving (shifting them to mean 0 and scaling them to an average distance of √2), which is supposed to help because A mixes 1s with numbers in the hundreds of thousands. The condition number of A was 107 to 108, but in float64 that didn't matter. The errors came out the same to within 0.01 px with and without it. I left it in since it doesn't hurt.

A homography that caught bad data

I originally had a third internet set, two photos of a bridge. Its H came out as (rounded):

H(bridge) = [ 2.0e-5   1.0002   -0.0660 ]
            [      0        0         1 ]

That's a pure 429 pixel shift sideways, with no rotation, scale or perspective. A real camera turning always bends the image at least a little, so the two "photos" were really two crops of the same picture. There's no projective transform to find, so I replaced that set with the cathedral.

Part A · A.3

Warping and rectification

If you warp forward, sending each source pixel to wherever H puts it, the pixels land between grid points and spread apart, which leaves holes. So I warp backwards:

  1. Send the 4 corners of the image through H to find the bounding box of the output.
  2. For every pixel in that box, send it back through H−1 to find where it came from in the source.
  3. Read a color at that spot. Pixels that come from outside the source get alpha 0.
Nearest neighbor rounds the source position to the closest pixel and copies it. Bilinear takes the 4 pixels around the source position and blends them, weighting each one by how close it is. Both are written from scratch with NumPy, with every pixel done at once and no Python loops over pixels. The output has an alpha channel that marks which pixels are real, which the mosaics use later.

Rectification: chessboard

This is a photo of a chessboard painted on an outdoor table, shot at an angle ("Chessboard 2" by Tiia Monto, CC BY-SA 4.0). A chessboard is made of squares, so I mapped it onto a flat 8×8 grid with 100 px squares.

Clicking the 4 corners works, but the outer border on this board is hand painted and wobbly, and 4 points leave no extra equations to average out mistakes. So I only used the 4 clicked corners to get a rough H. I used that to predict where the 49 inside corners should be, and then snapped each one to the exact spot where four squares meet, nearby. One corner snapped onto a seed lying on the board instead, and it got thrown out, which left 48 points.

Using only the 4 clicked corners, the inside corners land 5.13 px off from a perfect grid on average. With the 48 snapped corners the error is 0.82 px (max 1.97).

Rectification: building

The second one is a building from my Project 0 photos. I shot it looking up, so the walls lean in. I rectified one of the middle windows.

The tricky part is I don't know how tall that window is compared to how wide, and a wrong guess squishes the whole facade. Since I know the phone's focal length (24 mm equivalent, which is about 1387 px at the size I used), I could work it out. With the camera matrix K, the first two columns of K−1H point along the window's two edges in 3D, so the ratio of their lengths is the window's real height-to-width ratio. That came out to 1.22, and the angle between the two edges came out to 89.4°, which is a good check since a window's corner should be 90°.

The windows and the blue stripes come out square and straight, which makes sense since they're all on the same flat wall as the window I picked. The rock wall at the bottom is bumpy and sticks out from the facade, so it still looks a little warped.

Nearest neighbor vs bilinear

Nearest neighbor makes staircase edges. Every output pixel copies exactly one source pixel, so a straight edge at an angle turns into steps. You can see it along the edge of the square and the thin dark line under the ledge on the building. Bilinear blends neighbors, so the same edges come out smooth, but a tiny bit softer.

imageoutput sizenearest neighborbilinear
chessboard1101 × 11010.10 s0.27 s
building4862 × 55552.50 s8.11 s

Bilinear is about 3× slower, since it reads 4 pixels instead of 1 and does more math for each one. It's still only a few seconds even for a 27 megapixel output, so I used bilinear for everything after this.

Part A · A.4

Blending images into a mosaic

How I build a mosaic

  1. Pick one image as the reference (the middle one) and compute H from every other image into its frame, using the points from A.2.
  2. Send every image's corners through its H, and make one canvas big enough to hold all of them. Then shift everything so the canvas starts at (0, 0).
  3. Warp every image onto that canvas with bilinear warping, along with its alpha mask.
  4. Blend them together.

For blending, I give each image a weight that's 1 in its center and drops linearly to 0 at its edges, and warp that weight map along with the image. Then I compared three ways to combine them:

  • Overwrite: just paste each image on top of the last one. No blending at all.
  • Feather: every output pixel is the weighted average of all the images that cover it.
  • Two-band: split each warped image into low frequencies (a Gaussian blur, σ = 6) and high frequencies (the image minus the blur). The low frequencies get the weighted average, so color and brightness change smoothly. The high frequencies come only from whichever image has the biggest weight at that pixel, so edges stay sharp instead of doubling up. The blur only averages pixels inside each image's mask, so the black area outside an image doesn't leak in.

Gateway

Overwrite leaves hard seams on the ceiling and the floor, since each shot came out slightly brighter or darker. Feathering fixes those, but the chairs in the bottom right show up twice. They were close to me, and since I turned the phone in my hand instead of around the camera lens, the nearby chairs shifted against the background between shots. This is parallax, and no single H can line up both close and far things when it happens. Two-band keeps the chairs sharp, but you can still see where one chair switches from one image to the other. The far end of the room lines up fine in all three.

Looking back, I didn't turn far enough between shots. The two portrait shots both fit inside the width of the landscape one, so this mosaic mostly grows taller instead of wider.

Boat

This set lines up almost perfectly, since everything is far away and the points fit to under a third of a pixel. All three blends look clean here, even overwrite, because the three shots have very similar exposure. The images get stretched toward the left and right edges, which is just what a flat projection does to anything far from the middle. A.5 fixes that.

Cathedral

In the overwrite version you can see the edge of the second image across the ceiling, where its exposure is a bit different. Feather and two-band both hide it. Since this pair lines up so well, those two look almost the same, because there's nothing out of place for feathering to show twice.

Part A · A.5 · Bells & whistles

Cylindrical mosaic

This part is optional for CS180, but I did it anyway. A flat mosaic projects everything onto the plane of the reference image. That's fine for a narrow view, but the further you go from the middle the more things stretch, and an image 90° to the side would stretch out to infinity. Projecting onto a cylinder around the camera fixes that. Each column of the output is an angle θ around the camera and each row is a height on the cylinder, so turning the camera just slides the image sideways.

I build it backwards again, straight from the original photos. For each output pixel at angle θ and height v, I work out where that viewing direction hits the reference image's plane, which is x = f·tan θ, y = v / cos θ (measured from the image center). Then I send that point through H−1 into each source image and sample it with bilinear. That way every pixel only gets resampled once instead of twice, and I can reuse the same H's and the same two-band blend from A.4.

This needs the focal length f in pixels. The boat photos still have their EXIF data: a Canon 40D at 25 mm. The EXIF also gives the sensor's resolution (4438 pixels per inch), which turns 25 mm into 1798 px at the 1600 px width I used.

Compared to the flat version, the boat on the left and the buildings on the right aren't stretched sideways anymore, and the whole thing is 2516 px wide instead of 3030 for the same view. The tradeoff is that straight horizontal lines away from the horizon, like the bottom edge of the frame, curve a little. A cylinder keeps vertical lines straight but can't keep every straight line straight once the view gets wide.

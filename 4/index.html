Project 4 · CS180 · Fall 2026

Image Warping
and Mosaicing

In Part A I stitched photos into panoramas. I matched points between photos by hand, solved for the homography that maps one onto the other, warped the images with my own inverse warping code, and blended them into one mosaic. I also used the same warp to rectify a chessboard and a building so they look like they're seen head on. In Part B I made the stitching automatic: it finds corners, describes and matches them, and uses RANSAC to get the homography without any clicking.

  • homographies by least squares
  • nearest neighbor and bilinear warping from scratch
  • 3 mosaics plus a cylindrical one
  • automatic stitching with Harris, ANMS and RANSAC

Part A · A.1

Shooting the pictures

A homography only lines two photos up perfectly if the camera turns without moving, or if the scene is flat. So for each set the camera stays in one spot and just rotates, with a lot of overlap between shots.

Gateway is mine. I took it inside Gateway on my iPhone 16 Pro at 1×, turning the phone between shots. I took four, but the last one was almost the same view as the third, so I used three. The middle shot is landscape and the other two are portrait. The other two sets came from the internet, so I'd have three mosaics with different amounts of overlap.

Part A · A.2

Recovering homographies

A homography H is a 3×3 matrix that maps a point (x, y) in one image to (x′, y′) in another: [wx′, wy′, w] = H·[x, y, 1]. It has 9 numbers, but scaling all of them by the same amount gives the same mapping, so I fix the bottom right one to 1 and solve for the other 8, called h1 to h8.

Dividing out w and multiplying through gives two equations per point that are linear in h1 to h8:

[ x  y  1  0  0  0  -x·x'  -y·x' ]   [h1]     [ x' ]
[ 0  0  0  x  y  1  -x·y'  -y·y' ] · [h2]  =  [ y' ]
                                     [..]
                                     [h8]

Four points give exactly 8 equations, which is just enough, but my clicks are never perfect. So I used 7 to 13 points per pair, stacked them into a 2n×8 system Ah = b, and solved it with least squares (np.linalg.lstsq).

For picking points, I clicked rough matches by hand and then refined each one automatically. I take a 31×31 patch around the point in the first image, slide it around nearby in the second image, and keep the spot with the highest normalized cross-correlation, with a parabola fit through the peak to get sub-pixel accuracy. If even the best match was weak, I threw the point out. Those were usually on blank walls or repeating patterns like rows of windows, where lots of spots look the same.

Here's what the system looks like for Gateway 1610 → 1609. That pair has 10 points, so A is 20×8. The first point is (92, 542) in 1610, which matches (420.61, 390.93) in 1609, and it gives these two rows:

Part A · A.3

Warping and rectification

If you warp forward, sending each source pixel to wherever H puts it, the pixels land between grid points and spread apart, which leaves holes. So I warp backwards:

  1. Send the 4 corners of the image through H to find the bounding box of the output.
  2. For every pixel in that box, send it back through H−1 to find where it came from in the source.
  3. Read a color at that spot. Pixels that come from outside the source get alpha 0.
Nearest neighbor rounds the source position to the closest pixel and copies it. Bilinear takes the 4 pixels around the source position and blends them, weighting each one by how close it is. Both are written from scratch with NumPy, with every pixel done at once and no Python loops over pixels. The output has an alpha channel that marks which pixels are real, which the mosaics use later.

Rectification: chessboard

This is a photo of a chessboard painted on an outdoor table, shot at an angle ("Chessboard 2" by Tiia Monto, CC BY-SA 4.0). A chessboard is made of squares, so I mapped it onto a flat 8×8 grid with 100 px squares.

Clicking the 4 corners works, but the outer border on this board is hand painted and wobbly, and 4 points leave no extra equations to average out mistakes. So I only used the 4 clicked corners to get a rough H. I used that to predict where the 49 inside corners should be, and then snapped each one to the exact spot where four squares meet, nearby. One corner snapped onto a seed lying on the board instead, and it got thrown out, which left 48 points.

Using only the 4 clicked corners, the inside corners land 5.13 px off from a perfect grid on average. With the 48 snapped corners the error is 0.82 px (max 1.97).

Rectification: building

The second one is a building from my Project 0 photos. I shot it looking up, so the walls lean in. I rectified one of the middle windows.

The tricky part is I don't know how tall that window is compared to how wide, and a wrong guess squishes the whole facade. Since I know the phone's focal length (24 mm equivalent, which is about 1387 px at the size I used), I could work it out. With the camera matrix K, the first two columns of K−1H point along the window's two edges in 3D, so the ratio of their lengths is the window's real height-to-width ratio. That came out to 1.22, and the angle between the two edges came out to 89.4°, which is a good check since a window's corner should be 90°.

The windows and the blue stripes come out square and straight, which makes sense since they're all on the same flat wall as the window I picked. The rock wall at the bottom is bumpy and sticks out from the facade, so it still looks a little warped.

Nearest neighbor vs bilinear

Nearest neighbor makes staircase edges. Every output pixel copies exactly one source pixel, so a straight edge at an angle turns into steps. You can see it along the edge of the square and the thin dark line under the ledge on the building. Bilinear blends neighbors, so the same edges come out smooth, but a tiny bit softer.

imageoutput sizenearest neighborbilinear
chessboard1101 × 11010.10 s0.27 s
building4862 × 55552.50 s8.11 s

Bilinear is about 3× slower, since it reads 4 pixels instead of 1 and does more math for each one. It's still only a few seconds even for a 27 megapixel output, so I used bilinear for everything after this.

Part A · A.4

Blending images into a mosaic

How I build a mosaic

  1. Pick one image as the reference (the middle one) and compute H from every other image into its frame, using the points from A.2.
  2. Send every image's corners through its H, and make one canvas big enough to hold all of them. Then shift everything so the canvas starts at (0, 0).
  3. Warp every image onto that canvas with bilinear warping, along with its alpha mask.
  4. Blend them together.

For blending, I give each image a weight that's 1 in its center and drops linearly to 0 at its edges, and warp that weight map along with the image. Then I compared three ways to combine them:

  • Overwrite: just paste each image on top of the last one. No blending at all.
  • Feather: every output pixel is the weighted average of all the images that cover it.
  • Two-band: split each warped image into low frequencies (a Gaussian blur, σ = 6) and high frequencies (the image minus the blur). The low frequencies get the weighted average, so color and brightness change smoothly. The high frequencies come only from whichever image has the biggest weight at that pixel, so edges stay sharp instead of doubling up. The blur only averages pixels inside each image's mask, so the black area outside an image doesn't leak in.

Gateway

Overwrite leaves hard seams on the ceiling and the floor, since each shot came out slightly brighter or darker. Feathering fixes those, but the chairs in the bottom right show up twice. They were close to me, and since I turned the phone in my hand instead of around the camera lens, the nearby chairs shifted against the background between shots. This is parallax, and no single H can line up both close and far things when it happens. Two-band keeps the chairs sharp, but you can still see where one chair switches from one image to the other. The far end of the room lines up fine in all three.

Looking back, I didn't turn far enough between shots. The two portrait shots both fit inside the width of the landscape one, so this mosaic mostly grows taller instead of wider.

Boat

This set lines up almost perfectly, since everything is far away and the points fit to under a third of a pixel. All three blends look clean here, even overwrite, because the three shots have very similar exposure. The images get stretched toward the left and right edges, which is just what a flat projection does to anything far from the middle. A.5 fixes that.

Cathedral

In the overwrite version you can see the edge of the second image across the ceiling, where its exposure is a bit different. Feather and two-band both hide it. Since this pair lines up so well, those two look almost the same, because there's nothing out of place for feathering to show twice.

Part A · A.5 · Bells & whistles

Cylindrical mosaic

This part is optional for CS180, but I did it anyway. A flat mosaic projects everything onto the plane of the reference image. That's fine for a narrow view, but the further you go from the middle the more things stretch, and an image 90° to the side would stretch out to infinity. Projecting onto a cylinder around the camera fixes that. Each column of the output is an angle θ around the camera and each row is a height on the cylinder, so turning the camera just slides the image sideways.

I build it backwards again, straight from the original photos. For each output pixel at angle θ and height v, I work out where that viewing direction hits the reference image's plane, which is x = f·tan θ, y = v / cos θ (measured from the image center). Then I send that point through H−1 into each source image and sample it with bilinear. That way every pixel only gets resampled once instead of twice, and I can reuse the same H's and the same two-band blend from A.4.

This needs the focal length f in pixels. The boat photos still have their EXIF data: a Canon 40D at 25 mm. The EXIF also gives the sensor's resolution (4438 pixels per inch), which turns 25 mm into 1798 px at the 1600 px width I used.

Compared to the flat version, the boat on the left and the buildings on the right aren't stretched sideways anymore, and the whole thing is 2516 px wide instead of 3030 for the same view. The tradeoff is that straight horizontal lines away from the horizon, like the bottom edge of the frame, curve a little. A cylinder keeps vertical lines straight but can't keep every straight line straight once the view gets wide.

Part B · B.1

Harris corners and ANMS

Part B makes the same mosaics without me clicking anything. I followed the paper "Multi-Image Matching using Multi-Scale Oriented Patches" by Brown et al., with the simplifications from the spec.

The first step is finding points worth matching. The Harris detector slides a small window around each pixel and checks how much the brightness changes. A corner changes in every direction, so it scores high. The course gives sample code for this, but it uses scikit-image, which won't run on my laptop right now (SciPy broke after a macOS update). So I rewrote the same two steps with OpenCV: Sobel gradients, a Gaussian blur with σ = 1, the score 2·det / trace, and then every pixel that's the biggest in its 3×3 neighborhood counts as a corner.

That gives a lot of corners, 45,674 on this boat photo. The sample code keeps every local max, even tiny ones in the flat sky caused by noise.

Adaptive non-maximal suppression (ANMS) is the paper's fix for crowding. For every corner, it finds the distance to the nearest corner that's clearly stronger (stronger even after scaling it by crobust = 0.9). That distance is the corner's suppression radius, so a big radius means the corner is the strongest thing in a big area. Keeping the 500 corners with the biggest radii spreads them out evenly. Comparing every corner with every other one is a lot of pairs, so I sort them by strength first. Then each corner only needs to look at the ones ahead of it in the list, and I do the distances in chunks as one matrix multiply.

Run on every corner, ANMS spends most of its 500 points on empty sky and water. The noise corners there are isolated, so they get big radii. The paper avoids this by only keeping corners above a strength threshold (t = 10 in their units). Converted to my scale (brightness from 0 to 1 and an unnormalized Sobel), that's about 0.02. Only 4,527 corners on the boat photo are above it, and ANMS on those stays on real structure.

Taking the strongest 500 bunches them all along the skyline and the spire. ANMS keeps those strong spots but also spreads points into the clouds, the ice and the reflections, so any part of the photo that overlaps another one has something to match. The 500th point has a radius of 17 px.

Same thing indoors. The strongest 500 pile up on the windows and the counter at the far end, and ANMS spreads them over the pillar, the edge of the ceiling and the carpet.

Part B · B.2

Feature descriptors

Each of the 500 corners gets described by an 8×8 patch. I blur the image with a Gaussian (σ = 2) and sample it every 5 pixels around the corner, so the 64 samples cover the 40×40 window. The blur matters: sampling every 5 px without it would alias, and a blurry patch still matches if the corner gets found a pixel or two off in the other photo. Then each patch is normalized to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 (bias/gain normalization), so it still matches the same spot in a brighter or darker shot.

Each descriptor is a tiny, blurry version of its window. Number 6 is a dark wall with the bright strip of the embankment at the bottom, and its descriptor has a bright bottom row. Number 2 is almost flat water, but after normalizing its faint shading fills the whole range too. That's the downside of gain normalization: a patch with almost nothing in it gets its noise stretched up to full contrast, which makes patches like that easy to mismatch.

Part B · B.3

Feature matching

To match two images, I compare every descriptor in one with every descriptor in the other, using the squared distance between their 64 numbers. The closest is the 1-NN and the second closest is the 2-NN. The paper's point is that the 1-NN distance alone says little about whether a match is right, but the ratio 1-NN / 2-NN does. A real match is much closer than anything else, while a wrong match is barely closer than the runner up.

To pick the threshold I made my own version of the paper's Figure 6b. For boat 1→2, boat 3→2 and cathedral 1→2, I took all 1,500 features, found each one's nearest neighbor, and used my hand-picked homographies from Part A as the answer key. A match counts as correct if it lands within 5 px of where that H says it should.

Same shape as the paper: correct matches pile up near 0 and wrong ones near 1. Picking the threshold is a tradeoff between keeping correct matches (recall) and how many of the kept ones are correct (precision):

thresholdkeptcorrectprecisionrecall
0.332324776%61%
0.439129074%71%
0.545732471%80%
0.651234267%84%
0.760736861%90%

Wrong neighbors outnumber right ones almost 3 to 1, so even the flat low part of the orange curve adds up. I went with 0.5 on the squared ratio, which is about 0.71 on the plain distance ratio. It keeps 80% of the correct matches, and 71% of what it keeps is correct. Going lower barely helps precision (76% at 0.3) and throws away a lot of good matches, and RANSAC can handle 30% wrong matches fine.

Most lines run the same way, which is what the right answer looks like, and the ones going off at odd angles are wrong. Plenty of the wrong ones are hard to see though, since they land on a look-alike spot just a few pixels along the skyline or one window over. RANSAC has to deal with both kinds.

Part B · B.4

RANSAC and automatic mosaics

4-point RANSAC, written from scratch:

  1. Pick 4 random matches and compute the homography through them exactly (computeH from Part A).
  2. Send every match through that H and count how many land within 2 px of their partner. Those are the inliers.
  3. Repeat 5,000 times and keep the H with the most inliers.
  4. Refit H with least squares on all its inliers, recount them, and refit once more.

Even with 40% wrong matches, 4 random picks are all good 0.64 ≈ 13% of the time, so 5,000 tries find a clean sample hundreds of times over.

I first used 3 px for the inlier cutoff, and it broke boat 3→2: my clicked points from Part A landed 4 px off under its H. More than half of its inliers were chunks of ice on the river. Under the right H they're off by about 11 px on average, and 97% of them in the same direction, because the ice drifted with the current between the two shots. At 3 px, RANSAC found an in-between H that fit both the ice and the shore within 3 px, and since that covered more matches, it won. At 2 px the ice doesn't fit, and the H comes from the skyline and its reflection in the water, which don't move.

In Gateway almost every red line is on the floor or the chairs. Those were close to the camera and shifted with parallax, so they don't fit the same H as the far side of the room.

Manual vs automatic

They come out almost the same. The only differences I can see at this size are at the edges of the canvas. For example, the automatic Gateway canvas is 1600 × 1668 instead of 1600 × 1718. Out there both homographies are extrapolating past the last matched point, so small differences in H grow. The chairs in Gateway have the same parallax seam in both, since that comes from how I took the photos, not from the matching.

Part B · B.5 · Bells & whistles

Rotation, scale and panorama recognition

This part is extra for CS180. The spec says to pick one, but I did all three.

Rotation invariance and multiscale

The basic descriptor breaks if one photo is rotated or zoomed compared to the other, since the 8×8 grid ends up sampling a different part of the scene. I added both fixes from the paper.

Rotation: each corner gets an orientation from the image gradient after a big blur (σ = 4.5), and I sample the 8×8 grid turned to that angle. The same corner gets the same angle relative to the scene in both photos, so it gets the same patch.Multiscale: I build a Gaussian pyramid with 3 levels (blur, then halve), run Harris and ANMS on each level (500, 125 and 50 points), and sample each descriptor on the level where its corner was found. A corner from level 1 covers an 80×80 window of the original photo, so it can match a level 0 corner in a photo taken zoomed out by 2×.

To test them, I matched boat 1 against copies of boat 2 that I rotated and shrunk with my own warp from Part A. Since I made the copies, I know the right answer exactly: my clicked points in boat 2 go through the same rotation and scale. The error is how far the automatic H lands from them. Each cell is inliers and that error:

Each fix handles exactly its own problem. Rotated patches survive the rotation but not the zoom, the pyramid survives the zoom but not the rotation, and together they handle both. On the untouched pair, rotation costs a few inliers (95 vs 108). The orientation of a corner comes out slightly different in each photo, which turns its patch a little, and the upright version never has that problem. The rotated and shrunk test only gets 25 inliers because 0.6 falls between two pyramid levels, so the patch sizes never line up exactly.

Panorama recognition

Last, I gave it all ten images from this project in a random order, without telling it which ones go together: the three Gateway shots, three boat shots, two cathedral shots, and the building and chessboard from A.3 as distractors.

For each of the 45 pairs it matches rotated, multiscale features and runs RANSAC. A pair counts as overlapping if inliers > 8 + 0.3 × matches, the rule from Brown and Lowe's follow-up paper on automatic panoramas. Overlapping pairs get grouped together, each group uses the image with the most inliers to the others as its reference, and every other image gets connected to the reference through its strongest links, multiplying the homographies along the way.

It found the right groups by a wide margin. Every real pair had 63 to 129 inliers, and no wrong pair had more than 5. RANSAC can always find 4, since any 4 points fit some homography exactly, so 5 is basically nothing. Boat 1 and boat 3 even matched each other directly (63 inliers) through the part of the view they share. The building and the chessboard were left alone.
